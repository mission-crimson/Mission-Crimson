MISSION:CRIMSON

MISSION:CRIMSON is a browser-based survival game for children aged 7–12 that teaches them about Mars through gameplay. After crash-landing on Mars, the player becomes an astronaut who must find the 6 missing pieces of the spaceship, repair it and fly back to Earth.

Hostile Martians guard the planet with traps and puzzles: 43 yes/no space questions across three difficulty levels. Every wrong answer brings a Martian one step closer.

No download, no account: the game runs in any modern browser, on computers, tablets and phones.

How to play
Key	Action
Arrows / WASD	Move around Mars
Shift	Run faster (uses more oxygen)
E	Scan for the nearest part (−10 energy)
Space	Enter the base / launch the spaceship
Y / N	Answer YES / NO questions
P	Pause the game

On phones and tablets, on-screen buttons appear.

Gameplay

The game is built on three connected resources: oxygen, energy and health. No choice is free, so players must plan every trip from the base like a real mission.

Oxygen drops while exploring. Making more at the base costs energy, just like NASA's MOXIE experiment on Perseverance, which made oxygen from the Martian air using electricity.
Energy charges at the base only in daylight, through solar panels. It powers the scanner, oxygen production, food and calls to Earth.
Health drops with wrong answers and radiation. Eating and resting at the base restores it, but growing food under LED lights costs energy.
Martian traps and spaceship parts ask a yes/no question. A correct answer costs energy (−10 for a trap, −5 for a part). A wrong answer costs oxygen, energy or health, and the Martian moves closer. If the Martian catches the player, they return to the base and lose one collected part.
Coronal flares strike during the game, based on real events from NASA's DONKI space weather database. Players must find shelter quickly or lose health. One example is the solar storm of May 2024, when Curiosity recorded radiation equal to about 30 chest X-rays.
Calling Earth costs energy, and the answer arrives with the real Earth–Mars signal delay, calculated for the day you play.
Every game creates a new map, so each mission is different.
NASA data used and why
Source	How it is used in the game
NASA DONKI (Space Weather Database)	The game's coronal flares are real archived events. When a connection is available, the game also fetches the strongest solar flare of the last 30 days. Archived and live data are always labeled.
Curiosity RAD	Radiation on Mars: about 210 micrograys on a normal day and 8,100 micrograys during the May 2024 storm.
Perseverance MOXIE	Making oxygen from Martian carbon dioxide needs electricity: that is why oxygen costs energy.
Opportunity & InSight	The 2018 dust storm and dust on solar panels: energy on Mars is never guaranteed.
NASA Veggie	Plants grow in space under LED lights: that is why food costs energy.
NASA communication delay research	A message between Mars and Earth can take up to 44 minutes there and back.
NASA OSDR	Astronaut health (bone, muscle) and plants in space.
NASA Mars Exploration	Gravity, sol length, temperature, atmosphere, water and ice, moons, Olympus Mons, Valles Marineris.

Every question in the game links to the NASA page it comes from.

We acknowledge the Community Coordinated Modeling Center (CCMC) at Goddard Space Flight Center for the use of DONKI, https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/DONKI/.

Challenge relevance

The game turns NASA's open data into an interactive experience. Children meet the real hazards of Mars, such as the thin atmosphere, radiation, dust storms, oxygen production, food production and the signal delay from Earth (up to 22 minutes each way), as game mechanics rather than textbook facts.

Why it matters

Space education for young learners is often either too dry or too advanced. MISSION:CRIMSON makes space science fun, accessible and engaging, helping children build scientific knowledge, understand what it takes to survive on Mars and dream of becoming space explorers.

How it was built

MISSION:CRIMSON was conceived and designed by our team: the game idea, rules, mechanics, artwork, mission manual and every design decision are our own. We built and refined the code together with AI (Claude, by Anthropic), which helped us turn our design into a robust, polished and powerful game. We tested every part of the game ourselves, and every scientific fact was checked against NASA sources.
